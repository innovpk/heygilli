# HeyGilli — AI co-watching companion for kids' YouTube

**Name:** HeyGilli, decided 2026-09-05. The buddy character is **Gilli** the palm squirrel, from *gilehri*, the Urdu word for squirrel, and the little stick in gilli-danda. Kids call the app by talking to it: "Hey Gilli!". Gilli collects the words a child says like a squirrel collects nuts. Store check 2026-09-05: no app named HeyGilli or Hey Gilli on Google Play or the App Store; the only "Gilli" apps are gilli-danda games; heygilli.com and heygilli.io free (register today), heygilli.app and .pk taken. Pronounce with a short "i" (rhymes with silly); spelled with a double L so it never drifts toward *gaali*. Earlier working names rejected: Dekho (18+ streaming app, IPTV app, CarDekho), Mithu (existing AI parrot app), Jugnu (Pakistani B2B startup plus Jugnu Kids), Titli (existing kids app), HeyGilli (live short-video app on Play, Mac presence-coach app, all main domains taken), KidsTube/Kidsube (crowded, and "-tube" invites a YouTube affiliation objection), Goluvid (rhymes with Covid). ### 0.2 Why HeyGilli is an agent, not an app

The judging guidance is explicit: agents should run in the background and surface only when there is a real decision for a human. HeyGilli is the parent's agent, and the child-facing buddy is the visible end of it.

| Runs on its own | Surfaces to the parent only when |
|---|---|
| Watches approved channels for new uploads, screens each one for the kid's age band, generates the question plan | A new upload is borderline (topic drift, ads, scary content) and needs a yes or no |
| Runs the co-watching session end to end: pause, ask, listen, score, reply, resume, adapt (switches a pre-reader to pick-it after two silent answers) | A kid has been stuck on the same concept across three sessions |
| Writes the nightly digest and picks one dinner-table prompt | The daily time budget is about to run out and the kid is mid-video |
| Notices which channels never get watched and proposes trimming them | Never asks the parent to babysit the app |

That framing is the pitch for the Everyday Agents track: screen time turns from something a parent polices into something an agent handles, with a two-line report at the end of the day.

## 1. The pitch (30 seconds)

My six-year-old is subscribed to 148 YouTube channels. I had seen maybe ten of them. That is the
real problem: not that she watches, but that no parent can audit 148 channels, so nobody does.

HeyGilli reads the children's actual YouTube Kids subscription lists, the ones no API exposes, and
reviews every channel: what it publishes, whether anything on it is worth a look, and which ages it
suits. From then on an agent screens each new upload before it can reach them, and asks the parent
only about the borderline ones. The parent sets the limits and writes what happens during a break.
And while a child watches, a buddy pauses at natural moments and asks them about what they just saw,
so the minutes are not passive.

Ads still play. Creators still get paid. Nothing violates YouTube's terms.

**Positioning note.** The co-watching quiz loop is not the differentiator and must not lead the
pitch. Pausing a video to ask a question exists in QuizStop, Edpuzzle, PlayPosit and others. What
does not exist anywhere is reading a child's real YouTube Kids subscriptions and governing the whole
feed from them. HeyGilli is a governance product with a co-watching buddy inside it, not a quiz app
with settings.

## 2. Problem

- Kids aged 4 to 11 spend a large share of screen time on YouTube, and on the TV that time is completely passive.
- Parental controls filter content but do nothing during or after viewing. YouTube Kids is a filter, not a teacher.
- Parents want to know what their kid actually took in, not just what played.
- Bilingual households (Urdu and English) get no support from English-first products.

## 3. Users

| User | Age | Needs | Device |
|---|---|---|---|
| Child, pre-reader | 4 to 6 | Name things, copy sounds, point, be praised | TV first, tablet second |
| Child, early reader | 7 to 8 | Recall, simple why, predict; short text OK alongside voice | TV and tablet |
| Child, fluent | 9 to 11 | Explain, compare, apply; text questions fine, wants to be treated as older | Tablet first, TV second |
| Parent (buyer) | 28 to 45 | Safe catalog, proof of learning, less guilt about screen time | Phone |
| Sibling group | mixed ages | Shared TV session, questions pitched to whoever answers | TV |

Three age bands drive everything downstream: question types, answer inputs, timing, buddy tone, and the digest. The band is set from the kid's age at profile creation and can be overridden by the parent.

Design floor is the 4-year-old. They cannot read, cannot hold a mic button for a sentence, and will not wait more than a few seconds. If the pre-reader path needs text to proceed, the design has failed. Everything above that band adds capability; nothing below it is assumed.

## 4. Market and positioning

Researched 2026-09-05, revised 2026-09-06 after reading QuizStop properly.

**The quiz loop is crowded. Say so.** QuizStop (£2.99/month) pauses a YouTube or TikTok video, asks
a question, and takes voice, multiple-choice or drawn answers; it has family profiles for several
children and progress analytics with accuracy trends. Edpuzzle and PlayPosit have embedded questions
in videos for a decade. Kidzovo has an AI buddy over a licensed catalogue. A pitch built on "we
pause the video and ask a question" is indistinguishable from these, and a judge who knows any of
them will say so.

| Segment | Examples | Where HeyGilli differs |
|---|---|---|
| AI co-watching / video quizzing | QuizStop, Kidzovo, Coco AI, Edpuzzle, PlayPosit | They are **lesson-shaped**: an adult picks a video and turns it into a lesson. HeyGilli is **feed-shaped**: it governs the whole diet, continuously, without an adult curating each item |
| YouTube allowlist front ends | VidCove, Kivvie, WhitelistVideo, WatchSafe | They filter the catalogue but never tell a parent what a channel actually shows, and add no learning layer |
| Curated closed libraries | Kidoodle.TV, Lingokids, Khan Kids | The content kids actually want is not in them |
| Parental controls | Family Link, Kidslox, Bark | Time and blocking, no view into content, no learning |
| Platform | Gemini on Google TV (rolling out 2026) | No kids co-watching mode yet; watch closely |

**The three things nobody else does.**

1. **Reads the children's real YouTube Kids subscriptions.** No API exposes a YouTube Kids profile;
   the Google Takeout `children` export is the only route, and we found it. Measured on the author's
   own household: 148 channels for one child, 19 for the other, against 1 on the parent's own
   account. That gap is the product.
2. **Reviews the whole list.** 167 channels reviewed from their recent uploads in about five
   minutes for well under a dollar, surfacing three worth a look. A parent cannot do this by hand,
   which is exactly why nobody does it.
3. **Screens every new upload afterwards** and asks the parent only about the borderline ones.

**Two deliberate opposites to QuizStop**, aimed at different children. Theirs resumes only when the
child answers correctly, which is sound for speech repetition. HeyGilli never gates, never says
wrong, and treats silence as a teaching moment. And theirs is built for specialists and therapy;
HeyGilli is built for an ordinary household with a television and no time.

## 5. Product principles

1. **Voice-first.** Kid never reads instructions. Buddy speaks, kid speaks back. Pre-readers see no text at all; text appears as a companion to voice only from age 7.
2. **Never mock, never fail.** Wrong answers get curiosity, not correction. "Interesting! I thought it was because of the heat. Let's watch and see." Silence is never a failure either: for pre-readers the buddy models the answer and moves on.
3. **Interrupt lightly.** At most one question per 3 to 5 minutes. Questions land at natural breaks, never mid-sentence.
4. **The parent owns everything.** Parent account, kid profiles with no PII, parent gate on all settings.
5. **Play by YouTube's rules.** Official embed, ads shown, no overlays during playback, no downloads.
6. **Bilingual by default.** Buddy speaks the kid's language and code-switches naturally.
7. **Suggest, never enforce; and never claim more than the data supports.** The app cannot see a
   room, so it never tells a child what to do with their body and never pretends to check. What a
   break says is sentences a parent typed, read out verbatim; empty is a supported answer. A
   channel that drifts raises a card, it is never removed. A model can draft for a parent and can
   ask; only a parent decides, and whether a break even holds is a setting they own. The matching
   rule on screen: if a claim cannot be backed by the data on that screen, it does not get made.

## 6. User journeys

### 6.1 Parent setup (phone, 5 minutes)

1. Sign in with Google. Set a 4-digit parent PIN.
2. Create kid profile: name or nickname, age (sets the band: 4 to 6, 7 to 8, 9 to 11), language preference (Urdu / English / both), avatar.
3. Import channels. **Google Takeout is the primary path**, because it is the only route to a YouTube Kids profile's subscriptions. The parent exports YouTube data with the `children` and `subscriptions` categories, uploads the zip, and gets one card per child profile with its real channel list; profiles map to kids, and only the subscription CSVs are read. Secondary paths: import the parent's own account subscriptions (useful only when the kids watch on a shared account), paste a channel URL, or take a starter pack by age.
   Measured on the author's own export: the children's profiles held 148 and 19 channels; the parent's own Google account held 1. That ratio is why Takeout leads.
4. Choose question frequency (gentle / normal / off) and daily time budget.
5. Pair the TV: open HeyGilli on Google TV, enter the 6-digit code shown on the phone.

### 6.2 Kid session on TV, ages 7 to 11

1. Kid picks their avatar on the TV home screen. Home shows a row of "new from your channels" and a row of "continue watching". No search, no recommendations.
2. Kid selects a video with the remote. Buddy says hello by name and starts the video.
3. At the first natural break (see 7.3), the video pauses and shrinks to a corner. Buddy appears and asks a question aloud. The question is also shown as text, large for 7 to 8 and normal for 9 to 11. A mic icon pulses.
4. Kid holds the mic button on the remote and answers. Speech is transcribed and scored.
5. Buddy responds in one or two sentences, then the video resumes. If the kid says nothing for 8 seconds, the buddy says "No worries, let's keep watching" and resumes. Never blocks.
6. At the end of the video: "You watched a whole video about volcanoes! Want another, or shall we do something?" and an optional offline activity card.

### 6.3 Kid session on TV, ages 4 to 6 (pre-reader path)

1. Home screen is pictures only: big thumbnails, the kid's avatar, no labels. The buddy reads the video title aloud when a thumbnail is focused.
2. Kid selects a video. Buddy says hello by name and starts the video.
3. At the first break (later than for older kids, see 7.3), the video pauses on the current frame. The frame stays large, because the question is about what is on screen. The buddy appears small in a corner and asks aloud. No text anywhere.
4. The buddy asks one of three things: **name it** ("What animal is that?"), **copy it** ("Can you roar like him?"), or **pick it** (three big pictures appear; "Show me the red one"). For pick-it, the remote's left, centre, and right map to the three pictures, so a 4-year-old only has to press one direction.
5. Kid answers by speaking, copying, or pressing. Listening window is 5 seconds, not 8. A single word counts as a full answer.
6. **The buddy always models the answer**, whatever happened. Correct: "Yes! A giraffe. It has a looong neck" with a matching gesture from the buddy. Unclear or silent: "It's a giraffe! Gi-raffe. Can you say giraffe?" then a 3-second pause, then resume whether or not the kid repeats. The modeled word is the learning; silence is a teaching moment, not a dead end.
7. Video resumes. Videos under 5 minutes get one question. Longer videos get at most two.
8. At the end: "All done! You saw a giraffe and a red truck. Shall we watch one more?" Buddy names what they saw; no summary, no activity card beyond "can you find something red in your room?" spoken once.

### 6.4 Kid session on tablet

Same loops by band. Pre-readers get tap-the-picture as the primary answer for pick-it questions and the mic as a big single button for name-it and copy-it. Ages 7 and up can also draw. Lock mode via Android screen pinning.

### 6.5 Parent digest (phone, end of day)

Push notification, then a card. Its shape depends on the band.

Ages 7 to 11:

> **Rayan, today:** 42 min, 4 videos, 9 questions, 6 answered.
> **Understood:** volcanoes erupt when pressure builds. **Shaky:** why the moon changes shape.
> **Ask at dinner:** "What would happen if you shook a fizzy drink bottle and opened it?"

Ages 4 to 6:

> **Lisa, today:** 35 min, 5 videos, 6 questions.
> **Words Lisa said:** giraffe, red, three, truck.
> **Words she heard but didn't say yet:** hippo, purple.
> **Try today:** count the cars on the way to school.

For pre-readers the digest is a vocabulary and activity log, not a comprehension report.

## 7. The companion loop (core mechanic)

### 7.1 Pipeline per video

```
video selected
  → fetch metadata + transcript (see 9.4)
  → screen: is this OK for this kid? (age, topics)   [cached per video per age band]
  → generate question plan per age band: 1 to 2 questions for 4-6, 3 to 6 for 7-11,
      each with timestamp, type, input mode (voice | pick | copy), expected answer,
      acceptable variants, model-the-answer line, follow-up line, language variants,
      and for pick questions three picture options (one correct)
  → play; at each timestamp: pause → ask (TTS) → listen (STT or D-pad or tap) → score → respond → resume
  → end of video: summary line + optional activity
  → write session record for digest
```

The question plan is generated once when the video first appears on any kid's home screen and cached. Scoring happens live per answer.

### 7.2 Question types by age

| Age band | Types | Input | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 to 6 | Name it, copy it, pick it (colour, count to 5, animal, sound, feeling) | Single word by voice, D-pad left/centre/right, tap | "What animal is that?" "Can you roar like him?" "Show me the red one." |
| 7 to 8 | Recall, simple why, predict, sequence | Voice sentence, tap, draw | "Why did the ice melt?" "What do you think happens next?" |
| 9 to 11 | Explain, compare, apply, opinion with a reason | Voice sentence, typed short answer on tablet | "How is a volcano like a fizzy drink?" "Where else could you use that trick?" "Do you agree with him? Why?" |

Every question must be answerable from what was just watched. No trivia the video did not cover.

Pre-reader questions are about what is visible on the paused frame or what was heard in the last 30 seconds, never about causes. "Why" questions start at 7. For pick-it questions the plan generates three options where the two wrong ones are clearly different (a giraffe, a fish, a car), never near-misses.

Pictures for pick-it come from a fixed library of a few hundred kid-safe icons keyed by concept, not generated per video. The plan picks icon ids. This keeps latency at zero and avoids generated-image safety review.

Learning goals by band, which the question generator is told to serve:

| Band | Goals |
|---|---|
| 4 to 6 | Vocabulary, naming, colours, counting to 5, animal sounds, basic emotions, following a one-step instruction |
| 7 to 8 | Recall, cause and effect, prediction, sequencing, new words in context |
| 9 to 11 | Explanation, comparison, applying an idea elsewhere, forming an opinion with a reason, noticing when a video is trying to sell something |

### 7.3 Timing rules

| Rule | 4 to 6 | 7 to 8 | 9 to 11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| First question no earlier than | 2 min | 90 s | 90 s |
| Minimum gap, normal | 4 min | 3 min | 3 min |
| Minimum gap, gentle | 6 min | 5 min | 5 min |
| Max questions per video | 1 under 5 min, 2 above | 3 to 6 | 3 to 6 |
| Listening window | 5 s | 8 s | 8 s |
| Default frequency | gentle, cannot be raised | normal | normal |

- Place at a scene change, a sentence end in the transcript, or a music cue. Never inside a sentence.
- For 4 to 6, prefer moments where the subject of the question is clearly visible on the paused frame.
- Videos under 3 minutes get one question at the end only, for every band.
- Sibling mode: alternate bands between questions, and use the youngest band's timing rules.

### 7.4 Answer handling

- STT transcript, D-pad choice, or tap is scored against the expected answer and variants. Output: `correct | partial | off_topic | unclear | silence`. Pick-it answers score deterministically without a model call.
- Response is spoken, max 6 seconds of TTS. Everything the kid says is processed and discarded. Only the score and a 10-word paraphrase are stored.
- Never repeat a question more than once, in any band.

Ages 7 to 11:

- `correct`: warm confirmation plus one extra fact. `partial`: build on what they said. `off_topic`: accept it, gently redirect, resume. `unclear` or `silence`: reassure and resume.

Ages 4 to 6, where the "extra" is a word, a sound, or a motion, never a fact:

- Every outcome ends with the buddy saying the answer word clearly, once, with the buddy character doing a matching gesture on screen. This is the core learning event for this band.
- `correct`: "Yes! A giraffe. It has a looong neck." `partial` (for example "gaffe"): treat as correct, model the word: "A giraffe! Gi-raffe." `off_topic`: accept it, then model: "A dog? I see a giraffe! Gi-raffe." `unclear` or `silence`: "It's a giraffe! Gi-raffe. Can you say giraffe?" then 3 seconds, then resume regardless.
- Scoring is phonetic and forgiving. Any utterance that shares a first sound or a syllable with the expected word is `partial`, and `partial` is treated as success. Shy, whispered, and mumbled answers must not produce a different experience from confident ones.
- Copy-it questions ("roar like him") are never scored. Any sound or silence gets "Great roar!" or "Listen to mine, ROAR!" and resume.
- If the mic returns nothing twice in one session for a pre-reader, the plan switches the remaining questions to pick-it for that session.

### 7.5 Buddy voice and tone

- Named character, consistent voice per language.
- Curious, warm, slightly silly. Talks like a kind older cousin, not a teacher.
- Code-switches when the kid does. If the kid answers in Urdu to an English question, the buddy responds in Urdu.
- Hard rules baked into the system prompt: no sarcasm, no "wrong", no comparison to other kids, no mention of scores, never asks for personal information.
- Register shifts by band. 4 to 6: short sentences, stretched key words ("looong"), animal sounds, lots of "let's". 7 to 8: playful, curious, asks "what do you think?". 9 to 11: drops the baby talk entirely, talks like an older cousin who finds the topic genuinely interesting, and is allowed mild humour. An 11-year-old who feels talked down to will not answer a second question.
- The buddy is animated with a small set of gestures (stretch, shrink, spin, point, roar, think) that the reply can call by name. Gestures do the work that text cannot for pre-readers.

### 7.6 Five things the agents do that a filter cannot

Shipped after the core loop, and specified field by field in `docs/PROTOCOL.md` v1.7. Each one
exists because a household knows something the model does not, and each is built so the model
can only ever hand a parent a draft or a fact — never an action taken on their behalf.

**Household policy.** The Coach reads this child's own approved channels and drafts a handful of
questions about what is actually fine in this house: adverts inside videos, unboxing, mild peril,
challenge stunts, "just for laughs" cruelty. A parent answers or ignores them. A "rather not"
does not hide anything: it routes a matching video to the parent's inbox, so a screening decision
they cannot see never becomes a decision made without them. The payload carries `based_on` — the
channel titles the questions came from — so the screen can say whether these are this child's
questions or the generic set, rather than inferring it and getting it wrong.

**Watch history, opt in and counted on the way in.** A Takeout export contains every video a child
has watched. It is off by default, per import, with a tick that never remembers itself. Ticked,
`watch-history.html` is reduced to counts *while it is being read* — how many videos, which
channels, what times of day — and the file and every video title in it are then discarded. No
video title is stored or reaches a model; only channel names are. Search history has no opt-in
anywhere in the product. The headline it produces is the share that came from channels nobody
chose, which is the number a parent cannot get from YouTube itself.

**Channel drift.** A review is a snapshot, and channels change hands, chase trends, and start
running gambling ads two years after a parent approved them. Every approved channel is re-reviewed
weekly, and the comparison is made **in code**: `worse` means the verdict moved toward concern or
a flag appeared that was not there before. Only then is a model asked to describe the difference,
so a drift can never be talked into existence. A drift raises a card in the inbox and removes
nothing — that card has no Approve or Hide, because there is no video to decide about.

**Revisiting a shaky concept.** Analytics already knows what a child was shaky on. Acting on it is
one question in a later session, asked as a fresh question about the video they are watching now.
The failure mode is a child noticing they are being retested, so the protections are structural,
not prompt-level: at most one per session, never the first question, never two sessions running,
and nothing in what the device receives marks a question as a second attempt.

**Bilingual word seeding.** For a child whose languages include Urdu, Gilli may offer the Urdu
word for something they have *just shown they understood in English*, and ask for it back a
session later. This is the one place the product teaches rather than checks. **No model translates
anything**: the term is an exact lookup in `shared/icons.json`, the curated concept list that
already carries `en` and `ur` for every entry, because a confidently wrong word is worse than no
word. One per session, and never in place of a comprehension question.

## 9. Architecture

### 9.1 Overview

```
 Phone (parent)        Tablet (kid)          Google TV (kid)
   Flutter               Flutter                Flutter
      \                     |                     /
       └──────────── HTTPS + WebSocket ──────────┘
                            |
              Gateway (Python, FastAPI) — auth, pairing, session sockets
                            |
     ┌──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┐
     |   Strands Agents (Python SDK), on AgentCore Runtime   |
     |                                                        |
     |  Curator ──► Planner        Buddy (live)      Digest   |
     |  (scheduled Graph)          (per session)   (nightly)  |
     |                                                        |
     |  Tools: youtube_uploads, get_transcript, screen_video, |
     |         icon_lookup, score_answer, tts, notify_parent, |
     |         session_log, read_sessions                     |
     └──────────────────────┬──────────────────────┘
                            |
        Model provider (one line per agent, section 9.5)
        Bedrock (default) | Anthropic | Google | OpenAI | Ollama
                            |
     DynamoDB (profiles, channels, videos, plans, sessions, digests)
     AgentCore Memory (per-kid preferences: favourite topics, words said)
     YouTube Data API v3 · TTS provider · push notifications
```

One Flutter codebase, three form factors. TV uses a separate widget tree for D-pad focus and ten-foot layout; all models and services shared. Every model call goes through an agent; the client never holds a model key. The agent layer is Python because the Strands Python SDK is the more complete of the two (30+ community tools, Ollama and LiteLLM providers, first-class AgentCore starter toolkit) and BouncerAI already gave us a Python deployment habit.

### 9.2 Client (Flutter)

- `youtube_player_iframe` for playback on all three form factors. Pause and seek via the IFrame API. On pause, the player is scaled to a corner rather than covered, to respect embed policy.
- `speech_to_text` for the remote mic on Android TV and device mic on tablet. Push-to-talk, 8-second window for 7 and up, 5 seconds for 4 to 6. For pre-readers, the buddy also starts listening automatically after the question, so a kid who just talks at the TV without pressing anything is still heard.
- Pick-it answers on TV: three large pictures, remote left, centre, and right (and D-pad, and the number keys 1 to 3 where the remote has them). Focused picture wobbles. Any of the three presses answers; no confirm step.
- Icon library: a few hundred kid-safe concept icons bundled in the app, keyed by id, so pick-it renders instantly and never depends on the network or generated images.
- Buddy animation: a small Lottie or Rive character with named gestures. Reply payloads carry a gesture id.
- TTS: cloud voice (consistent character voice, Urdu support) streamed from the backend, with `flutter_tts` as offline fallback. For 4 to 6 the buddy's speaking rate is reduced and key words are emphasised via SSML.
- TV: leanback launcher intent, TV banner, all navigation via D-pad. No touch assumptions. Pre-reader home screen is thumbnails only, with the title read aloud on focus.
- Tablet: screen pinning lock mode; three-picture tap answers; big single mic button; simple draw canvas for 7 and up; short typed answers for 9 to 11.
- Pairing: TV shows a 6-digit code, phone claims it, backend links the device to the household.

### 9.3 Agent service (Python, Strands Agents SDK)

Four agents, each a Strands `Agent` with a system prompt, a model, and a small tool set. Tools are plain Python functions decorated as Strands tools.

| Agent | Trigger | Tools | Output | Autonomy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Curator** | Scheduled, every 6 hours per household, and on channel add | `youtube_uploads`, `get_transcript`, `screen_video`, `notify_parent` | Approve, hide, or ask-parent per new video | Decides alone on clear cases; asks the parent only on borderline ones |
| **Planner** | Called by Curator for each approved video, once per age band and language | `get_transcript`, `icon_lookup`, `save_plan` | `QuestionPlan` (structured output, section 10) | Fully autonomous; plan is cached |
| **Buddy** | Live, one instance per session, driven over the gateway's WebSocket | `score_answer` (deterministic for pick-it, model for voice), `tts`, `session_log`, `switch_mode` | Ask, reply, resume messages (section 11) | Adapts mid-session: pick-it fallback, language switch, skips a question if the kid is restless |
| **Digest** | Nightly per kid, and on demand | `read_sessions`, `read_memory`, `notify_parent` | `Digest` (structured output) plus an optional parent alert | Decides whether anything is worth a notification at all |

- **Orchestration.** Curator → Planner is a Strands **Graph**: deterministic edges, Planner fans out per band. Buddy and Digest run standalone. No Swarm; nothing here needs model-driven handoffs, and deterministic edges are easier to demo and explain.
- **Gateway.** FastAPI. REST for auth, profiles, channels, pairing. One WebSocket per session that forwards client events to the Buddy agent and streams its ask/reply/resume messages back. Latency matters here more than anywhere: the gateway keeps the Buddy agent warm for the session rather than cold-starting per turn.
- **Deployment.** Agents on **AgentCore Runtime** via the starter toolkit; the gateway on the same AWS account (App Runner or Lambda with WebSocket API Gateway). Kid preferences (favourite topics, words said, language mix) in **AgentCore Memory** so Gilli can say "you liked the giraffe yesterday". Fallback if AgentCore fights us in week one: run the same agents in-process inside the FastAPI service on App Runner, keep the Strands code identical, and say so in the README.
- **Storage.** DynamoDB for everything in section 10. Single table, PK by household. Fits the $50 credits and the AWS judging lens. Postgres stays an option post-hackathon.
- **Schedules.** EventBridge rules for Curator and Digest.
- **Everything the model touches is logged** with Strands' built-in tracing so the video can show the agent's tool calls on screen for a few seconds.

### 9.4 YouTube integration and the transcript problem

- **The children's real subscriptions:** a Google Takeout export, `children/<profile>/subscriptions.csv`. No API reaches YouTube Kids profiles: Family Link exposes none (there is only an open feature request), the People API's relations field is unrelated contact data, and the Data API only ever sees the signed-in account. Watch and search history are present in the same export and are deliberately never read.
- **Scope discipline:** sign-in asks only for identity. `youtube.readonly` is a *sensitive* scope, so it is requested incrementally, only when a parent chooses to import their own account's subscriptions. Most households never grant it, which keeps the app out of sensitive-scope verification and out of the weekly refresh-token expiry that applies while an app is in Testing.
- **Catalog:** YouTube Data API v3. `subscriptions.list` for the parent's subscriptions, `channels` → uploads playlist → `playlistItems` for new videos. Quota is 10k units/day; cache aggressively.
- **Playback:** official IFrame embed only. Ads play. No overlays while playing. No autoplay tricks.
- **Transcript:** this is the one hard integration decision. `captions.download` in the Data API only works for videos the caller owns, so it is useless here. Options, in order of preference:
  1. **Video-understanding model that accepts a YouTube URL directly.** Gemini's API does this for public videos and is the only fully official path. It lives inside the `get_transcript` tool, not as an agent's model: the tool produces a timestamped transcript summary and scene breaks, and the Planner agent, on whatever provider is configured, does the rest. A Bedrock-only build uses option 2 inside the same tool.
  2. **Public caption track fetch** via the timedtext endpoint that the web player uses. Works for most videos, no auth, but unofficial and can break. Acceptable as a hackathon fallback, not as a product foundation.
  3. **Creator-supplied transcripts** for partner channels later.
- Videos with no usable transcript are still playable; the buddy asks only generic end-of-video questions ("What was your favourite part?").

### 9.5 Model layer: provider-flexible by design

Strands is model-agnostic: the provider is one constructor argument on the agent, and switching between Bedrock, Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Ollama, or LiteLLM is a package install and one line. HeyGilli leans on that deliberately. Each agent reads its provider and model id from configuration, so the same code runs against different vendors without edits.

```
# Verified on this AWS account in us-east-1 on 2026-09-05 (cross-region inference profiles)
HEYGILLI_MODEL_CURATOR=bedrock:us.anthropic.claude-sonnet-4-6
HEYGILLI_MODEL_PLANNER=bedrock:us.anthropic.claude-opus-4-8
HEYGILLI_MODEL_BUDDY=bedrock:us.anthropic.claude-haiku-4-5-20251001-v1:0
HEYGILLI_MODEL_DIGEST=bedrock:us.anthropic.claude-sonnet-4-6
AWS_REGION=us-east-1
HEYGILLI_INGEST=google:gemini            # tool-level, see 9.4; or captions for Bedrock-only
```

| Role | Default for the hackathon | Why | Alternates, one line each |
|---|---|---|---|
| Curator, Planner, Digest | Claude on **Amazon Bedrock**, newest Claude available in the chosen region | AWS judging lens, structured output, strong at kid-safe tone | Anthropic direct (`claude-opus-5`), OpenAI, Google |
| Buddy (live replies, voice scoring) | The fastest Claude on Bedrock in region | A child is waiting; latency beats depth here | Anthropic direct, Ollama with a small model for offline dev |
| Ingestion tool | Gemini via the Google API (YouTube URL in, transcript out) | Only official way to read a public YouTube video | Caption fetch (unofficial) for a Bedrock-only demo |
| Pick-it scoring | No model | Deterministic | n/a |

Rules that hold on any provider:

- **Structured output** for the question plan, the score, and the digest, via Strands structured output with Pydantic models. The client never parses free text.
- **Frozen system prompts** per agent and band, with the per-video transcript appended once, so provider-side prompt caching works where the provider offers it.
- **Effort and thinking** are provider-specific knobs set in one place per role: low for Buddy, high for Planner and Digest, whatever the provider calls them. On Anthropic direct that is adaptive thinking plus `effort`; on Bedrock the equivalent request fields; on providers without them, nothing.
- **Every provider must pass the same eval**: 20 transcripts × 3 bands × 2 languages → plans checked for band rules (no "why" under 7, pick-it options clearly distinct, timing table respected) and 60 recorded child answers → scores checked against a human label. Run it once per provider before the video. This is what makes "flexible" a fact rather than a claim.
- **The demo shows the swap**: one config change, redeploy, same session runs. Ten seconds of video, large credibility gain on Technical Implementation.

Model ids above were read from `aws bedrock list-inference-profiles --region us-east-1` on day 1 and a live `converse` call on Sonnet 4.6 returned successfully, so Bedrock access is confirmed on the existing account. Also available there: `us.anthropic.claude-fable-5` and `us.anthropic.claude-opus-4-7`. The account's default region is ap-southeast-1, so every Bedrock call passes us-east-1 explicitly. Ollama with llama3.2:3b is installed locally as the key-free provider for tests. **Update, later on 2026-09-05:** after the first successful call, every Anthropic model on Bedrock began returning `ResourceNotFoundException: Model use case details have not been submitted for this account` (Sonnet 4.6, Haiku 4.5) or `AccessDeniedException` (Opus 4.8). Action for Mujahid: Bedrock console → us-east-1 → Model access → Anthropic → submit the use-case form; approval is usually same day. Until then the agents run on `bedrock:us.amazon.nova-pro-v1:0`, which supports Strands tools and structured output and passed 15 of 18 eval cells; its 4_6 pick-it drafts are weak and get repaired or dropped by the rules layer. Set `HEYGILLI_MODEL_DEFAULT=bedrock:us.amazon.nova-pro-v1:0` for now and switch back to the Anthropic ids with no code change once access is approved.

Rough cost per 10-minute video, first viewing (plan) plus one kid's session (6 answers):

| Step | Calls | Approx tokens | Approx cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ingestion (video model) | 1 | provider-specific | ~$0.01 to 0.05 |
| Question plan | 1 | 6k in / 1.5k out | ~$0.07 |
| Scoring + reply | 6 | 3k in (mostly cached) / 100 out each | ~$0.02 |

Under $0.15 per video-session. Subsequent kids watching the same video skip the plan cost.

### 9.6 Speech

- STT: on-device Android SpeechRecognizer via `speech_to_text` (free, fast, supports Urdu on most Google TV devices). Fallback: stream audio to a cloud STT if on-device Urdu quality is poor on the demo device. Test this in week one.
- Child speech is the hard case. Recognisers are trained mostly on adults; 4-year-olds mumble, whisper, and mispronounce. Mitigations: pass the expected word and variants as recognition hints where the engine supports them; score phonetically on the backend rather than on exact transcript; treat any utterance as at least `partial` for pre-readers; fall back to pick-it after two empty results. Test with a real 4-year-old on day 5, not with an adult imitating one.
- TTS: cloud voice with a fixed character voice per language, cached per line where possible (greetings, reassurance lines). Question and reply lines generated per turn.

## 12. Compliance and safety

- **COPPA and Play "Designed for Families":** parent account model, no ads of our own, no third-party SDKs that track, no child PII, voice processed and discarded, privacy policy and data deletion in the parent app.
- **YouTube API Services terms:** official embed, ads untouched, no download or offline caching of media, no overlays while playing, quota respected, attribution retained.
- **Content safety:** allowlist is a hard gate. Anything not approved is invisible even if a subscribed channel uploads it. Pre-screening of new uploads flags rather than auto-approves.
- **Model safety:** system prompt forbids personal questions, scary content, and any request for the child to do something in the real world beyond the offline activity cards, which are parent-visible.
- **Parent gate** on every settings screen and on exiting kid mode.
